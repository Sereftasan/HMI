1a. Using it by touch

A panel with a touch screen can be used with a finger. Whether this PC's screen has a touch function is said once, by an Admin, on HMI Settings › Options (9b): Touch screen On. Only then is the mouse / finger button (🖱 / 👆) on the caption bar, its menu choosing how the screen is used now – with a mouse or by touch – and Users and Preferences has Screen used with (everyone's, or a user's own, taken when they sign in, Users and Preferences); the screen is used by touch while that says Touch, or after the button is switched to it. Touch screen Off – a screen with no touch function – the program is used with a mouse, and neither the button nor Screen used with is shown. By touch:

  • A finger's size. Menu entries, table rows, tick boxes and round options, toolbar buttons and tabs are larger – back as they were with a mouse.
  • A held button is held. Windows does not turn a finger held on the screen into a right-click, nor a finger drag into its own pan: a jog button moves while the finger is on it and stops when it lifts, as with a mouse. A hold-to-run button (a jog, a cylinder's button) is pressed the moment the finger touches it – it does not wait for Windows to decide what the finger does.
  • A long press (the finger held still for about half a second) where a right-click opens a menu – a table's headings or rows, a side bar button, a PLC Monitor tab, a module's value – opens that menu under the finger; lifting it chooses nothing. A jog or set button, a slider, a splitter or a chart never opens a menu that way. A table with a row menu also has a ⋮ at the end of the chosen row: a tap opens it.
  • Tooltips. Where a long press opens no menu, it shows the tooltip beside the finger – what a mouse resting there shows; lifting the finger then presses nothing. (Tooltips do not come by themselves in touch mode: a finger rests nowhere.) A hold-to-run button shows none: holding it is the movement.
  • A tap on something greyed says why, beside the finger – the reason its tooltip gives with a mouse (your level, the machine not in manual, a controller not answering …).
  • A closed list (one you choose from, not type into) opens as a menu of large entries.
  • A finger drag scrolls: a table's rows and columns, a list, a page too large for the screen. A tap chooses a row (two taps are a double click); a drag does not choose the row it started on, and a button it starts on is not pressed. A finger's small tremble does not move the page back. A slider, a jog button, a splitter, a chart, a table's headings and a tab strip are not scrolled that way – there the drag is the work.
  • Grips on the borders. The side page's border is wider and carries three grips (its middle and near each end); a side page in a window of its own has a wider frame with a grip at each corner and at the middle of each side. Hold one and drag: the side page or the window is sized as it goes (a corner sizes both of its sides). With a mouse the grips are not there and the borders are thin again.
  • Typing. A tap on a text box brings a keyboard on the screen, always in the same place: along the bottom of the screen, across its whole width. While it is there the program is laid out as on a screen that much lower – the navigation panel, the page and the side page end at its top and scroll above it – the box you type into is scrolled into sight just above it, and a dialog it would cover moves up above it. All of it goes back when the keyboard goes. Which keyboard: the menu of the mouse / finger button on the top bar, On-Screen Keyboard – everyone's, or a user's own (Users and Preferences, its table):
    • Windows' – Windows' On-Screen Keyboard (the default): a whole PC keyboard – every sign, Tab, Esc, Ctrl, the arrows – in Windows' input language (the Turkish Q layout when it is Turkish). The program starts it along the bottom of the screen; once it is open, Windows lets no ordinary program move it or close it, so it stays until its own close button is tapped (a program run as an administrator closes it when you leave the box). Dragged up to float over the page, it leaves the program the whole screen. On a computer without it, the HMI's own keyboard comes.
    • HMI's – the HMI's own keyboard: the Turkish Q layout (ı i ğ ü ş ö ç, İ and I as Turkish writes them), the digits along the top, a page of signs, the arrows, backspace (held, it repeats), Enter, and ✕ to put it away. It goes when you leave the box, tap ✕, or Enter in a one-line box.
    • Off – no keyboard on the screen: for a panel used with a keyboard of keys. A number still brings the keypad.
    A number – a number cell of a table, a box with arrows – gets the keypad (5) with its limits; its OK writes it in.
  • A double click's work has a button of its own on the operator's pages (the notes' View, …); Find in the notes opens a line on a tap.

The touch screen is switched on for the panel; Screen used with appears at once on Users and Preferences, no restart. While it is off, a user's Screen used with is kept, and taken again once it is on.

Anyone may read the notes; writing one needs someone signed in.

4. Manual Movement: the units

Every cylinder, motor and axis you can move by hand is a unit: a frame with its name on the top line, one or two buttons, and sometimes a position reading between them. Axis units and cylinder units are the same size and shape so a page reads as one table, and every position reads the same way: black bold figures on grey, as on the Axis Settings rows.

A machine's own Manual Movement and Machine Parameters pages - their tabs, rows and sections - are described in its chapter (13. This machine). So is the machine's own bar above Manual Movement's tabs, when it has one – the axes' speed, JOG and the jog steps, say, and Automatic ON/OFF (below).

The buttons

  • Momentary (jog): the movement runs only while you hold the button. It stops when you let go – and also if the pointer is taken away by a message, the page changes, the button is hidden or disabled, or you are signed out.
  • Alternate: one press on, the next off (motors, cooling, blowing).
  • A button turns darker while its relay is on, and darker still with white text while the physical output is on.
  • The small dots on a button's outer corner are its sensors; rest the pointer on one to see which.
  • Its alarm: rest the pointer on a button – after what it does, its tooltip names the alarm bit that reports its movement failed, with the alarm's text (ON now while it is set); an axis unit's buttons add the drive's code words (servo alarm, axis alarm, axis warning). The unit's frame lists the alarm bits of both its movements, each by the movement's name. Any button can name one (its ErrorAddress).
  • A slot with no function stays as an empty grey cell, so the buttons of neighbouring units line up.
  • An axis unit has its Axis Settings row's arrows – ▼ ▲ for a vertical axis, ◀ ▶ for the others – and shows its position alone (the other positions are on the Axis Settings pages and the Axis Monitor).
Safety. A jog is released by the HMI in every case it can detect. The machine's own interlocks, emergency stop and the PLC program remain the protection – never rely on the screen to stop a movement.

Coupled units

Maintenance Units of the same kind can be coupled to move together – several clamps, several axes – as if their buttons were pressed at the same time. Coupling is for Maintenance and Admin users (an Operator sees no chain), on Manual Movement, Machine Parameters › Axis and the Axis Monitor; another page opened lets every coupling go.

  • The chain at the right end of a unit's top line (its ? left of it) couples it: the chain closes and the frame turns blue. Click it again to uncouple it; with three or more coupled it offers Uncouple '<unit>' or Uncouple all. One unit left alone is no coupling.
  • Cylinders couple with cylinders, axes with axes – and only those that can move together: cylinders with a sensor at each end standing at the same end (all back, or all forward); cylinders whose position is read (a pneumatic positioning, an encoder, an analogue sensor) with the same feedback, wherever each one stands; axes on the same page, wherever each one stands, and only while the axes are in step mode (the machine's JOG off – JOG chosen meanwhile lets the coupling go at the next press).
  • While units are coupled, the chains of the units that cannot join are faded and do nothing – a click says why. With nothing coupled, a chain is faded while its unit cannot be coupled at all (between its ends, its position not read, on another page).
  • Press a button of one coupled unit: the same button of every coupled unit is pressed – held while you hold it, let go when you let go. A click on a button that switches on clicks theirs.
  • Before every movement the units are checked again: still on the screen, still at the same place, their button usable now. If one is not, the coupling is let go, the chain says why, and only the unit you pressed moves.
  • A unit that leaves the screen (another page or tab) leaves the coupling; a single unit left is no coupling. Signing in or out lets every coupling go.
  • While units are coupled, the caption bar shows a blue chain, blinking (steady with Attention marks: Steady): its menu lists the coupled units – a click on one uncouples it – and last Uncouple all.
Safety. Each coupled button keeps its own conditions and level. The units are checked before a movement starts, not while it runs: watch every coupled unit while they move.

A cylinder back and forth: Automatic ON/OFF

Maintenance Automatic ON/OFF – a button of the machine's bar above Manual Movement's tabs – is a function of the HMI, for testing and setting up (it has no PLC bit of its own). While it is on (its lamp lit), a press of a cylinder unit's button – a cylinder, a pneumatic positioning, a cylinder with an encoder; not an axis, not a motor – starts that cylinder moving back and forth by itself: that side's command on and the other side's off, then the other way round (a spring valve: its coil on, then off). Its coupled units move with it.

  • Each way lasts half a second less than the shortest alarm delay of the units moving together that way – a movement's own Cylinder delay when one is entered, else the machine's Cylinder alarm delay (5 s: 4.5 s), never under half a second (10): time to arrive, and no alarm. The two ways may differ; the unit says both when they do.
  • It stops – both sides off – when a button of the unit is pressed again, Automatic ON/OFF is switched off, another user signs in, the unit leaves the screen, one of its commands can no longer act, or Manual Movement is closed.
  • The button is shown on Manual Movement only, for Maintenance and Admin: the same bar on the Axis page and the Axis Monitor has no Automatic ON/OFF.
Safety. A cylinder moving back and forth goes on with nobody holding a button. Stay at the machine, keep clear of what moves, and switch Automatic ON/OFF off before you leave the page.

4a. Pneumatic positionings

A cylinder the PLC drives to a position – a 5/3 valve and a position sensor, used as an axis is – is one row, laid out like a row of Axis Settings: its name at the left end, then its sections under their headings.

  • Manual movement – its two buttons (◀ ▶, or ▼ ▲ for a vertical one) and its position: a unit as above, without the frame. Its chain and its ? stand at the right end of the section; its alarms, its help and its coupling are any unit's.
  • Target position – where it goes, and ▶| beside it, which starts the positioning (the PLC switches it off).
  • Calibration – the calibration position, and ▶| beside it, which calibrates: where the cylinder stands now is taken as that position.
  • Positioning tolerance – how far from the target still counts as there; Direction ref – the direction reference (some pages called it the stopping offset): the PLC compares the target with it to choose the way to go – to a target above it the cylinder moves the way the position increases, to a target below it the other way.
  • The target and its start are the Operator's; the calibration, calibrating, the tolerance and the direction reference need Maintenance. Every value written and every press is in the log.
  • A positioning the machine has only with an option is shown only while the option is on; the rows under it move up, so the page has no empty lines. The rows share one names' column, as wide as the widest name.
  • The page is a table, as an axis page is: the column names stay at the top while the rows scroll up and down, and the names stay at the left while they scroll sideways (at a larger screen scale a row is wider than the screen) – which cylinder a value is of is always in sight.
Safety. Calibrating tells the PLC where the cylinder is. Calibrate only with the cylinder standing where its calibration position says it is – a wrong calibration sends every later positioning to the wrong place.

4b. Cylinders with an encoder

A clamp or a stop whose position an encoder counts (its reference sensor sets the count) looks on a Manual Movement page like any unit – its name, its two buttons with their sensors, its position between them, its chain and its ?. Its buttons, sensors, position, valve and feedback, error bits and helpers come from that row; the unit on the page has only the encoder's number, its title on that page (the row's name when it has none) and the conditions it is shown under there.

  • A unit whose encoder has no row says so in its title (Encoder 12: no encoder settings row) and does nothing.
  • The encoders page is a table like a page of positionings: its column names stay at the top, its names at the left.
  • A cylinder unit placed by hand has no position of its own: a cylinder whose position is read is a pneumatic positioning (4a) or a cylinder with an encoder.

5. Entering a value

  • Touch a value field: the keypad opens beside it and the field is outlined in blue so you can see which one you are changing. By touch (1a) a table's number cell and a number box with arrows get the keypad too, and a text box the keyboard on the screen.
  • The line at the top gives the allowed range. A value outside it is refused (the entry flashes red) – nothing is sent.
  • Angles (the angle axes' settings) are always 0 – 360°. A minimum above the maximum is a range through zero: minimum 270, maximum 45 allows 270…360 and 0…45, and the line reads 270.000 ≤ … or … ≤ 45.000. A minimum below the maximum is an ordinary range. The field blinks when its value is outside, as any limited field does.
  • The first key replaces the whole value; ◀ ▶ move into it to change one digit.
  • Use the keypad's . key for decimals – on any Windows language setting. On a keyboard both . and , type the decimal point.
  • Values are typed in the unit shown on screen (mm or inch, °C or °F); the HMI converts them for the PLC.
  • Some fields ask for confirmation before writing.
  • A speed drawn as a slider (the Home page's speed): one frame – its caption, a slider, and the percentage at its right end. Drag the slider (or tap it) through its steps – equally apart, green up to the value – and the value is written when you let go; ← → step it. Touch the percentage for the keypad: any whole value from the first step to the last. A value set elsewhere is shown as it is. Without its level, or with its controller not answering, it is grey and writes nothing; its tooltip says why.

Each of them writes exactly as the keypad does: the same limits, security level and confirmation.

6a. Alarms the HMI raises itself

Two switches per machine, in Alarms (10). With the cylinder alarms switched on, the HMI does not wait for the PLC to report a cylinder that did not arrive: it times every cylinder unit's movements itself. The axis and inverter alarms – on unless switched off – watch every axis's drive and every motor's inverter.

  • When the time starts: when the output that drives the movement says so – for a double solenoid valve, that movement's own coil output coming on; for a single solenoid (spring) valve, the coil output coming on for the coil's movement and going off for the spring's. The output is the one the unit's command drives on the drawings (or the unit's own output address); a unit with neither is timed from its command. A movement that was already commanded when the HMI started is not timed.
  • What confirms it: its feedback – the sensor at its end on and the one at its start off; a single sensor going off for the end it does not see; a clamp's pressure switch; a position reading that moved and settled (an analogue sensor, an encoder away from its reference). A unit with no sensor is not timed.
  • What is not a failure: a 5/3 valve's output let go of before the end (a button released), a spring valve's output changed back, a movement reversed by the other coil. A 5/2 double solenoid valve keeps going after a short pulse, and is timed on.
  • Not confirmed within its delay: the movement's alarm, with a sub-number for the signal that failed – like a drive's 50.1, 50.2:
    CodeWhat failed
    M840.1The start sensor stayed on: the cylinder did not leave its start (or that sensor did not go off)
    M840.2The target sensor did not come on: it stopped on the way, or the sensor does not see it
    M840.3The target sensor came on, but the start sensor stayed on
    M840.4Both coils were on: the valve cannot switch
    M840.5The position reading did not change
    M840.6The position reading was still changing
    The number before the point is the movement's error bit (its command bit when it has none). On the alarm list the alarm shows its own code and text, with what failed in the Cause column; the code with its sub-number is in the Address column, for an Admin. The alarm goes by itself when the movement is confirmed after all, or when it is acknowledged. Its help is the step-by-step page for that failure (4).
  • An axis (a row of Axis Settings), with the axis alarms on: its alarm as soon as its Servo Alarm or Axis Alarm shows a code other than 0 – no delay – with the code as the sub-number (M801.16.3, M801.1904H), until the code clears. An Axis Warning is only a warning: it raises nothing. What the axis was doing when the code came is kept with it for its help (4) – with the axis alarms off too.
  • A motor (4c), with the same switch: its alarm as soon as its inverter's fault code is not 0, with the fault as the inverter's panel names it (M830.E.OC1), until the code clears.
  • The PLC is told: when the HMI raises an alarm it SETs its device – the movement's or the axis' error bit (M840, M801, a motor's M830) – so the PLC program knows of it; the PLC's alarm reset clears it, as for its own alarms. The Address column shows that device alone (M801); the sub-number is part of the code (M801.16.3), not of the address. A movement or an axis with no error bit is not written to. Each write is in the PLC write log (HMI alarm M801.16.3: its error bit set).
  • One fault, one line: while the HMI's alarm for a device is listed, the PLC's own alarm bit for the same device is not listed beside it. Acknowledged while the PLC's bit is still on, the PLC's alarm then shows as usual.

6b. Messages from the PLC

The machine's PLC can ask the operator something, or tell him something, on the HMI: No profile has reached the profile recognition unit, Wrong profile, The axes are referencing…. The message opens over the main window, in the HMI's language. Its band carries the machine and the message's number, in red for an error, orange for a warning, blue for a question and dark blue for information.

  • Answer with its buttons – OK, Yes / No, Continue / Cancel, Restart / Cancel. Enter is the first button, Esc the Cancel or No. The answer goes to the PLC and the message closes. An answer is a command: it needs someone signed in at the controller's level – otherwise the buttons are grey, with Login beside them.
  • Some messages show values read from the PLC while they are open: Wrong profile the defined and the measured width and height, Servo out of limits each axis with its Needs reference and Fault lamps.
  • The pages keep working behind it. It stays until it is answered or the PLC takes it back; a new message from the same PLC replaces it.
  • A message with no buttons (Referencing) is the PLC telling its own state: Hide only hides it; it closes when the PLC is done.
  • If the answer does not reach the PLC, the message stays and says so (The answer did not reach the PLC, and why): answer again.
  • Every message shown, answered (with the answer and by whom) and closed is in the audit log; the answer's writes are in the PLC write log (PLC message 2: Yes).

How it works: the PLC writes the message's number to the machine's PopupTriggerAddress (set by the machine's program, see its chapter in 13). A button writes its answer to PopupResponseAddress – Cancel / No 0, OK / Yes / Continue 1, Restart / Retry 2 – and then 0 to the trigger. The messages, in every language, are in the machine's Popups.json; a number that is not in it is shown as PLC message n, with OK.

7. PLC connection

At the end of each controller's row on HMI Settings › PLC Connections, under PLC data: Read… Maintenance and Write… Admin – only while the controller is connected.

11. Logs and diagnostics

  • Every change made from the HMI is written to the audit log with the time and the user: one file per day, HmiAudit_yyyy-MM-dd.txt.

11a. The machine's log, taken by the HMI

A machine's log – its states, its production events and its alarms, with their values – is kept by its PLC in a log area and taken from there into the machine's log database (main_log, log_details), which the Dashboard, its reports and the Alarms page's History read. The Industry 4 service, a Windows service of its own, did that. With Industry 4 logging in the HMI switched on (9b) the HMI does it, record for record as the service did, on its own connection to each machine's first PLC – the service is no longer needed.

  • One logger per machine with a log database and a state map, started with the main window. Its state is on the machine's Machine Parameters › Alarms and Logs and on HMI Settings › Options: Taking the records from PLC station 1 (R27000): 120 written since the start, Waiting for the PLC: not connected, Switched off …
  • The log area: a word device of the PLC (R27000) – its first word the records waiting, the next the HMI's answer, the records from its tenth word, ten words each. Each machine has its own, set on its Alarms and Logs (empty: the one the machine's program gives).
  • Each record is a row of the database, with the PLC's date, and its values as details – divided as the state map says, 32-bit values from two words, each named bit that was on a detail of its own. The answer to the PLC is written with it: if it is refused, nothing is kept and the PLC sends it again. The answers are not in the PLC write log (a handshake for every record, not a user's write); the audit log says when a logger starts.
  • A record's Process ID can be looked up in KMSystem (PostgreSQL) – its Profile Code and Profile Data added – when switched on, through Npgsql.dll beside the program, as the service had it.
  • While the Industry 4 service runs on the PC the HMI waits and says so (Waiting: the Industry 4 service runs on this PC …): both would take the same records. Stop the service, or uninstall it.
  • The HMI's own log in the same database. While the HMI logs, every entry of its log – sign-ins, connection events, writes, settings, problems – and every device of the PLC write log is also a row of the main machine's log database, with codes of its own (1000 other, 1001 sign-in, 1002 connection, 1003 a control's write, 1004 a problem, 1005 settings, 1006 a device written; the machine's own are 0 – 157). The daily files are still written first, and brought into the database at the start. The dashboard, its counts and the reports leave the HMI's rows out – the report files are as they were; All logs and the log tables read them back.

The dashboard

The Dashboard page (the gauge in the page menu, on a machine that has one) shows what the machine did over a day, a week, a shift: its states, its events, its alarms and its cuts, from the machine's Industry 4 log – the database the logging service, or the HMI (11a), writes (main_log and log_details).
